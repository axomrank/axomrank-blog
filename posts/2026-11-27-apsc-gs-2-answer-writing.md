**For APSC Mains GS-II answer writing, start with the question's verb, explain the relevant rule or institution, and use an Assam example to show how it works.** GS-II is listed as Paper III in the Mains syllabus. It covers governance, Constitution, polity, social justice and international relations. These three original prompts focus on federalism, local government and e-governance.

## Centre–State relations and flood management

**Practice prompt:** Explain how cooperative federalism can improve flood management in the Brahmaputra basin.

**How to structure it.** State why a shared river creates a coordination problem. Explain a constitutional mechanism and a specific statutory institution. End with a practical improvement that different authorities could verify.

**Model answer**

Flood risk in the Brahmaputra basin crosses state boundaries. Upstream rainfall, river flows and sediment can affect districts elsewhere in the basin. Planning therefore needs to bring together authorities that manage different stretches of the river and agencies that respond to floods.

Article 263 provides a framework for a council that can discuss issues of common interest and recommend better coordination. The Brahmaputra Board Act, 1980 gives a specific statutory example. It establishes a board with a mandate that includes preparing a master plan addressing floods, erosion and drainage. Assam can use basin-level planning alongside district-level decisions about evacuation routes, warning systems and shelters.

Cooperation should produce specific responsibilities. Agencies can share river data in a common format and agree which office issues warnings at each stage. A pre-monsoon review can then test whether upstream information reached district officials in time to act.

## Local government and effective devolution

**Practice prompt:** Why does elected representation need to be backed by funds and administrative capacity for rural local government to work in Assam?

**How to structure it.** Separate powers, finance and staffing. Name the tiers that would perform a function. Explain who checks results and acknowledge the different framework in specified tribal areas.

**Model answer**

Elected rural bodies give residents a way to influence local priorities. To turn those priorities into services, the bodies need clear responsibilities, money to carry them out and staff who can plan and supervise the work.

Article 243G allows a state legislature to give Panchayats powers and responsibilities, including planning for development and social justice. Article 243I provides for a State Finance Commission to review Panchayat finances and make recommendations. In areas covered by the Assam Panchayat Act, a Gaon Panchayat can identify local needs, an Anchalik Panchayat can help coordinate work across villages and a Zilla Parishad can address district-level priorities. Specified Sixth Schedule areas have a different constitutional framework.

A drinking-water repair illustrates the connection. Assign the local institution responsibility for repairs, budget for maintenance and publish service records for residents to review. That gives elected representatives the means to act and gives residents a way to judge the result.

## E-governance and Sewa Setu

**Practice prompt:** Assess how Sewa Setu can strengthen accountability in Assam's public service delivery.

**How to structure it.** Explain how a portal can make a decision traceable. Connect that feature to accountability under the state law. Identify who might still struggle and propose a measure of service quality.

**Model answer**

Sewa Setu can make an application traceable from submission to decision. An acknowledgement gives the applicant a reference to use in later enquiries. A status record helps show whether a case is pending, whether documents are missing or whether a decision has been recorded.

The Assam Right to Public Services framework adds an accountability mechanism for notified services. It provides for service delivery within a stipulated time and a route to appeal in applicable cases. Tracking helps an applicant document delays, while the appeal route gives them a way to challenge the handling of the application.

Access still depends on practical details such as connectivity, language and the ability to upload documents. Assisted submissions and clear reasons for rejection would make the system more usable. To assess whether it is working, compare how consistently applications meet the notified timelines and whether applicants can resolve delays through the available process.

## Review your own answer

- Underline the sentence that answers the prompt directly.
- Circle each Article, Act or institution you named. Check whether you explained what it does.
- Mark where the Assam example supports your argument. Add a consequence or limitation if the example reads like a name dropped into the answer.
- End with an action, a trade-off or a way to judge delivery.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is APSC Mains GS-II the same as Prelims GS-II?

Mains GS-II is Paper III on governance, Constitution, polity, social justice and international relations. Prelims GS-II is the aptitude paper described in the [APSC CSAT guide]({{base}}/apsc-csat-syllabus-practice/).

### When should I include an Assam example in a GS-II answer?

Use one when it helps explain the question, especially for topics where the syllabus emphasises Assam. Show what the institution does or which problem the example illustrates.

### How do I improve a weak conclusion?

Return to the question's demand and give the reader a specific consequence, reform or measure of success. For e-governance, timely completion and access to an appeal are more informative measures than the number of applications alone.
